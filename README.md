## Overview

The Ahool is a cryptid reported from the mountainous rainforests of western Java, Indonesia. Its name is generally treated as onomatopoeic, imitating a drawn-out 'a-hooooool' cry that witnesses describe carrying through river valleys at night. The most-cited reports come from the Dutch-Indonesian naturalist Ernest Bartels, son of ornithologist M. E. G. Bartels: in 1925 Bartels reported being passed overhead by an enormous unknown flying creature near a waterfall on the Salak Mountains, and around 11:30 PM on a night in 1927 he heard the same loud 'a-hool' call from his hut near the Tjidjenkol River. Bartels' notes were taken up after his death by the cryptozoologist Ivan T. Sanderson, who promoted the Ahool in his 1948 article 'There Could Be Dinosaurs' and his 1967 book Living Mammals of the World as a candidate for an unknown giant bat or surviving primitive flying primate. Witness accounts give it a wingspan in the range of 3 to 3.7 m (10–12 ft), grey fur, a flattened, ape- or owl-like face, and clawed forelimbs; it is said to skim rivers at night to catch large fish. Mainstream zoologists generally explain the reports as misidentifications of large flying foxes (Pteropus vampyrus, the Indo-Malayan flying fox) or owls; no specimen, photograph, or remains has ever been produced.

## Theories & Explanations

### Scientific

**Undescribed giant bat** _(Ernest Bartels, Ivan T. Sanderson)_

Bartels and Sanderson interpreted the Ahool as a possible undescribed species of very large bat, taxonomically distinct from any known megabat.

**Surviving primitive flying primate** _(Sanderson and later cryptozoologists)_

An alternative cryptozoological hypothesis suggests the Ahool is not a bat at all but a relict population of an unknown gliding or flying primate, paralleling the Olitiau and other tropical flying-creature cryptids.

### Skeptical

**Misidentified flying fox** _(Mainstream Indonesian zoologists)_

The Indo-Malayan flying fox (Pteropus vampyrus), the largest bat in the region, has a wingspan up to ~1.5 m and is plausibly enlarged in eyewitness memory. Most zoologists treat Ahool reports as misidentifications of this species.

**Misidentified large owl**

The Javan eagle-owl (Bubo sumatranus) and other large owls produce loud calls and broad silhouettes at night and are sometimes invoked as more parsimonious explanations.
